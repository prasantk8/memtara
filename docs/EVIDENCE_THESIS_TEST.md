# Evidence Thesis Test

Companion to `docs/AI_INCIDENT_EVIDENCE_LIBRARY.md`. That document maps eleven real cases
against our six `DecisionEvidence` blocks. This document scores our product thesis against
those eleven cases and states plainly what the distribution implies.

**The thesis being tested:** that verifiable, per-decision AI evidence — independently
checkable proof that defined governance checks were applied to a specific AI-assisted
decision — is something institutions should buy.

**What we are not allowed to claim, restated:** we do not prevent bad decisions, bad
policies, or bad executives. We do not prove a control "operated." We produce independently
verifiable evidence that defined governance checks were applied to a specific decision —
nothing more.

---

## The three columns

### Column 1 — Evidence would have materially changed the outcome or the defense (1 of 11)

**Cigna** (`Kisting-Leung v. Cigna`, E.D. Cal. 2:23-cv-01477). The governance failure and the
evidence that exposed it are the same fact: a 1.2-second average review duration, discovered
by an outside reporter years after the practice began. A mandatory, signed `human_review`
duration field — visible to Cigna's own compliance function from month one, not
reconstructible after the fact — would plausibly have surfaced this as an internal anomaly
before it became a 300,000-claim, multi-year pattern requiring investigative journalism and
federal litigation to establish. This is the one case where the record itself, not just the
existence of an evidence product, would likely have changed something about *how the practice
was allowed to continue*, not merely how fast it was later proven.

*Marginal case, discussed but not counted in Column 1:* UnitedHealth/naviHealth shares the
same shape (a coerced `human_review` step, exposed by an external pattern rather than caught
internally) and a mandatory, non-suppressible `override` field would likely have surfaced the
same kind of early statistical anomaly. It is placed in Column 2 below because the
disciplinary coercion itself lived in HR and management channels a decision record cannot
capture — the record would surface the *symptom* fast, not the *mechanism*, which is a
meaningfully weaker claim than Cigna's.

### Column 2 — Evidence would have changed only the speed and cost of proving what happened (8 of 11)

**Zillow, Rite Aid, UnitedHealth/naviHealth, Workday, iTutorGroup, Air Canada, Foodinho,
DoNotPay.**

The pattern across all eight: the harm-producing decision was made by people or systems with
either the authority (Zillow's executives, iTutorGroup's screening design) or the operating
latitude (Rite Aid's store staff, Air Canada's ungrounded chatbot, Foodinho's dispatch
algorithm, Workday's scoring model across many customers, DoNotPay's unvalidated outputs) to
cause the harm regardless of whether a record of it existed. In every one of these eight,
evidence would have compressed years of investigation, discovery motions, or regulatory
fact-finding into a fast, per-decision, independently checkable record — materially changing
*who could prove what, how fast, and without needing the company's cooperation*. It would not
have changed whether the harmful decision was made in the first place.

Worth stating plainly, because it cuts against a naive sales pitch: in **iTutorGroup**,
faithful per-decision evidence would have been actively unhelpful to the company, not
protective. A signed record proving "the model applied the configured age threshold exactly
as designed, on every one of 200+ applicants" is not a defense — it is a clean, aggregable
exhibit for disparate-treatment liability, arguably a *worse* position than an unrecorded,
harder-to-aggregate pattern a plaintiff has to reconstruct claim by claim. Evidence
infrastructure proves that defined governance checks were applied; it is indifferent to
whether those checks were themselves lawful. This is the honest version of Column 2 for at
least one case: not "we'd help you defend yourself faster," but "we'd help someone else prove
you did it, faster and more completely."

### Column 3 — Evidence would have changed nothing (2 of 11)

**Delphia, Global Predictions.** Both are "AI-washing" cases: the marketed AI capability did
not exist as a functioning pipeline. A `DecisionEvidence` record is *generated by* a real
decision pipeline; there is no retrospective evidence-tooling fix for a capability that was
never built. The only way evidence infrastructure becomes relevant to this failure mode is
prospective and contractual — a counterparty requiring proof-of-pipeline as a condition of
engagement — which is a due-diligence use case for someone else buying from a company like
Delphia, not a protection for the company itself.

## What the distribution implies

**8 of 11 in the middle column is the finding, not a caveat.** If our sales narrative implies
that verifiable per-decision evidence meaningfully changes outcomes in AI governance failures,
that narrative is supported by one case out of eleven researched here, and even that one
(Cigna) required the evidence to function as an *internal* early-warning mechanism nobody was
required to look at — not as a courtroom defense. The realistic, honest claim is: **we reduce
the time and cost of finding out what happened, and we shift who is able to find out** (a
regulator, a plaintiff, an internal compliance function, or a counterparty, without needing
the subject company's cooperation or a multi-year discovery process). That is a real value
proposition. It is a materially smaller one than "AI evidence infrastructure prevents these
outcomes," and we should not imply the larger one.

**Who our buyer really is, and isn't.** The distribution says our buyer is not primarily "the
company trying to avoid the Zillow/iTutorGroup outcome" — for a company planning to do the
thing that gets Zillow or iTutorGroup sued, faithful evidence is neutral-to-harmful to them,
not protective (see iTutorGroup above). Our buyer is the institution that:
(a) genuinely wants its own controls to be real and is willing to find out fast if they
aren't (the Cigna counterfactual — an internal compliance or risk function that would act on
an early signal if given one), or
(b) needs to prove, to a regulator, an auditor, or a counterparty, that its controls *are*
real, faster and more cheaply than by producing witnesses and documents under discovery (the
Column 2 pattern), or
(c) is a counterparty doing due diligence on *someone else's* AI claims (the Delphia/Global
Predictions counterfactual — a due-diligence buyer, not the AI-washing company itself).

None of these three is "buy this and your AI governance failures stop happening." All three
are defensible. Only the first genuinely resembles a compliance-prevention pitch, and it is
one case out of eleven, contingent on someone inside the buying organization actually watching
the signal we'd surface.

**What we should stop claiming.** Any language that implies verifiable evidence changes
whether harm occurs, rather than what can be proven about it afterward and how fast. The
correct framing is consistently: *independently verifiable evidence that defined governance
checks were applied to a specific AI-assisted decision* — not that the checks were good
checks, not that the outcome was good, and not that we would have stopped anything.

## The observation that points back at us

The source document's own concluding observation, item 6 of its recommended controls: "legal
and technical review of every AI capability statement; substantiation audits matching
marketing claims against the operational codebase." The document then notes that the SEC
fined two investment advisers a combined $400,000 (Delphia $225,000 + Global Predictions
$175,000, SEC Release No. IA-6535 and companion order, Mar. 18, 2024) for describing AI
capability that did not exist — not for having bad AI, but for the gap between what was
claimed and what was built.

**The implication for us, specifically:** every sentence on the AIHOOTS site is a capability
statement about cryptography — what gets proven, what gets verified independently, what a
third party can check without trusting us. Per the `decision_evidence_spec.md` gap analysis
(Task 1), roughly 14 of the ~30 leaf fields the reviewer specified for `DecisionEvidence` are
fully missing from the codebase today — concentrated almost entirely in `model.*` (all 7
sub-fields) and `human_review.*` (all 5 sub-fields), the two blocks that carry the most weight
in nine of the eleven cases in this library. If our public claims describe a system that
captures model identity and human review per decision, and the current codebase does not yet
capture either, that gap is structurally the same shape as the gap the SEC fined Delphia and
Global Predictions for — a described capability ahead of a built one. This is not a reason to
understate what the cryptographic proof pipeline genuinely does today (per the spec, the
`bb verify` + committed-vkey path is real, independently checkable, and the strongest part of
the system) — it is a reason to make sure every claim about `model` and `human_review`
capture is scoped to what exists now, not what the schema in Task 1 describes as a target.
Before any public-facing capability claim ships, it should be checked against the same
question a bank's general counsel would ask: *if I asked you to produce one, right now, would
you be able to?*
